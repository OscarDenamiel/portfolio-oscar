The hook

Stayforlong, an OTA specializing in long-stay rentals, had been deferring its mobile app for years. The engineering team was small. No native developers. No budget. And the web mobile experience was fundamentally broken: fragmented UI patterns, no design system, navigation that failed unpredictably.

By April 2026, just 9 months after the launch, the app became 20% of the company's total gross GMV, increased mobile conversion 66%, and raised customer LTV 17% year-over-year.

This is how a small team, tight constraints, and strategic insights turned a years-old gap into a growth engine.

A broken web mobile experience

In mid-2025, Stayforlong's web mobile was losing market share to competitors like Booking and Airbnb. The conversion rate hovered around 1.5%, half the desktop rate. But the real problem wasn't the platform, it was how the platform was built.

My heuristic evaluation revealed:

No design system. Components were inconsistent. Button styles varied. Ad-hoc variables.

Broken navigation. The browser's back button didn't work predictably. Users got stuck in loops.

Browser-dependent patterns. Features that worked in Chrome failed in Safari.

No mobile-first thinking. The UI was a compressed desktop redesign, not a mobile-native experience.

Hidden core actions. The four actions users cared about most (search, view bookings, account, and support) were scattered behind menus and nested views.

AHA MOMENT

The constraint that became a catalyst

Here's where the story could have ended: we need a native iOS and Android app, but we can't build it.

The engineering team: David (EM), Carlos (Staff FE), and Jesús Gándara (contractor). No native developers. No budget for Reskyt, the SaaS wrapper solution other startups were using. No capacity to hire.

But in June 2025, our CTO watched a demo of a pattern he'd seen before: a PWA wrapped in a native shell using Expo and React Native. Take the responsive web app, wrap it with native capabilities (push notifications, app store integration, offline support), and ship it.

The insight was simple: we can build this ourselves. We already have a responsive web. The wrapper is a solved problem. The constraints forced us to be rigorous in deciding what really mattered.

"We didn't build a native app. We made the web so good it deserved to be one."

MY ROLE

3 decisions that made the app work

I joined as Lead Product Designer before kickoff. I began with a heuristic evaluation that revealed 4 non-negotiables for the app to work: consistent navigation, bottom navigation with 4 core actions, progressive disclosure, and persistent UI elements. These weren't design preferences. They were survival requirements. From them emerged 3 foundational decisions.

Decision 1
Persistent navigation system

I defined a fixed navigation model (bottom nav + consistent back behavior) to make core actions always reachable in one tap.

Decision 2
Progressive disclosure

I made the next booking step always visible, improved information hierarchy and removed endless scroll from HDP.

Decision 3
Architecture shift

I drove the move from a monolithic frontend to a hexagonal architecture, separating business logic from UI and enabling a scalable design system.

DECISION 1

Persistent navigation system

Web mobile navigation was broken. Back behavior depended on browser quirks. Core actions were hidden behind menus. HDP was endless scroll. Users got lost, couldn't recover, bounced before booking.

Hamburger menu

Hide nav, reclaim space. Problem: users hunt for it every context switch. Works for secondary navigation, not core actions.

Persistent tabs

Show tabs at top, always visible. Problem: 6-inch screens have no real estate. Tabs don't signal "you are here" reliably across flows.

Bottom navigation ✓

Fixed bar, always visible, thumb-friendly. The iOS/Android standard. On Stayforlong, the 4 main actions were obvious: search, bookings, help, account.

Before — hamburger menu, scattered core actions, broken back behavior

After — persistent bottom navigation, 4 core actions always visible

Why bottom navigation won

On apps, users expect persistent, always-visible navigation. A hamburger menu is a web convention — users accept hunting for it. On an app, it feels broken. A user shouldn't need three taps to jump from search to bookings.

We tested early: 85% of users preferred bottom nav. Persistence beat hidden menus every time. Time to task reduced significantly.

Why this was an app enabler: Core actions always reachable in one tap. Navigation became predictable and learnable. Reduced support contacts for "how do I find X". It became the anchor for every other pattern: CTAs, filters, bottom-sheets, modals.

The result: Users knew where to go. Navigation became invisible. The bottom bar became our design system's signature.

DECISION 2

Progressive disclosure

HDP was an endless scroll. Everything above the fold because we had nowhere else to put it. Users scrolled through reviews, amenities, policies, and bounced before reaching the booking button.

Show everything

Users get lost in information. They bounce before reaching critical content (rooms, pricing, booking button).

Expandable sections

Users don't interact with accordions. Feels like mobile web, not app.

Native patterns ✓

Bottomsheets for room details, modals for filters, accordions for amenities. Show summary, hide complexity until tapped.

Before — HDP endless scroll, all information above the fold

After — progressive disclosure, bottomsheets, clear hierarchy

Why progressive disclosure won

On a 6-inch phone, you can't show everything at once. Progressive disclosure isn't optional — it's how mobile apps function. It reduces cognitive load. Users see what matters, when they need it. Native apps don't show everything. They guide through steps. That's what users expect.

The result: HDP went from endless scroll to information hierarchy. Conversion improved because the booking step was always visible. This tied directly to the +28% CR uplift on SERP cards with visible CTAs.

DECISION 3

Architecture shift — monolithic to hexagonal

There was a design system in Figma, but it didn't exist in production. UI and business logic were fused together. A Button component carried state, API calls, business rules. You couldn't reuse it without duplicating code or creating tangled dependencies. We couldn't scale.

Keep monolithic

Faster short-term, but each feature increases complexity. We'd never ship a proper app — just a web wrapper.

Separate UI from logic

More upfront work, but true reusability. Business logic independent from UI.

Hexagonal architecture ✓

Pure design system (visual atoms only), UIKit layer (context-aware components with business logic), core logic completely isolated. Ports and adapters at the edges.

This wasn't just a technical improvement. It was what enabled us to scale beyond the app — future platforms, faster iteration, reduced tech debt. True component reusability across web and app. Business logic testable and swappable without breaking UI.

Hexagonal architecture: Design System (visual atoms) → UIKit (business logic) → Core (isolated). Web, app and future platforms share the same foundation.

EXTERNAL CONSTRAINT

iOS compliance challenge

iOS rejected us three times when we tried to publish the app on the App Store. Each rejection was feedback, not a blocker.

Rejection 1 — Missing ATT consent

Apple requires explicit permission before cookies track. We designed a GDPR/ATT consent flow that met both regulations.

Rejection 2 — No Sign in with Apple

We had Google Sign-in, but Apple wanted theirs. I made a strategic choice: temporarily removed Google Sign-in, shipped email/password only, committed to adding it later. This unblocked us.

Rejection 3 — No account deletion

I designed the flow, coordinated backend, shipped in one week. Each stung. But I reframed them: not obstacles, requirements. Apple was telling us what users needed. We listened.

"The app feels like a web wrapped in a shell."

Post-launch usability testing, Q4 2025

Research-driven iteration: from functional to native

Post-launch research revealed we had solved usability, but not perception. Users could tell it wasn't native. So I ran a second design pass focused entirely on native micro-experiences: custom onboarding, gesture-based interactions, native-style animations, loading states that signal "app."

This wasn't a redesign. It was a refinement. But necessary. A functional app that feels like a web wrapper is fragile. Users prefer the web. By making it feel native (in experience, if not in code), we made it be native, increasing trust and repeat usage.

THE EXECUTION

Speed over perfection

Android shipped in 6 weeks. Not because we worked overtime. Because we were ruthless about scope.

Android — 6 weeks to public launch

Jun 12: Channel created. Reskyt evaluated and rejected. Jun 26: Kickoff. Goal: MVP by end Q3. Jul 25: PoC with tracking, internal testing. Aug 1: Beta on Google Play. Sep 1: Public launch. Google's more permissive. We shipped MVP, not perfection. Iterated on feedback.

iOS — 6 months, 3 rejections, 1 tradeoff

Jan 18, 2026: approved after months of back and forth. Jan 25, 2026: sessions explode — 2,000 to 13,600 in 7 days. The delay was the cost of Apple's bar. But the tradeoff taught us: constraints force clarity. We shipped faster. Users adapted.

THE HARD PART

The moment that almost broke us

The "almost failure" wasn't one thing. It was three things colliding.

Iterable failed (Aug–Oct 2025)

Cookie tracking broken. Notifications never sent. Users weren't re-engaged. Bookings stalled. I focused on the UX for the problem at hand: users who received no notifications. Made opting in feel urgent, not spammy. We re-engaged manually until Iterable fixed their side.

Apple's three rejections

iOS delayed 6 months. We watched competitors gain ground. I embraced the constraint. Each rejection was feedback. Instead of fighting requirements, I designed for them. Removed Google Sign-in. Shipped faster. Added it back later.

The psychological weight

Small team. No safety net. One bad App Store review felt existential. CEO and CTO sent clear signals: "You're not failing, you're figuring it out." When iOS launched and sessions exploded 6x, the validation was immediate.

LEARNINGS

What I'd do differently

Anticipate Apple's requirements upfront

I now know exactly what Apple asks for: ATT consent, Sign in with Apple, in-app account deletion, privacy policy, data handling flows. On my next app, I'd have all assets ready before submission 1. Cost: 1–2 weeks prep. Benefit: 6 weeks saved.

Propose the wrapper solution earlier

I should have connected the dots faster. The CTO had the PWA insight. I had the heuristic evidence. We didn't align until June. If I had: we'd have shipped in May, not September.

Reframe rejections as design requirements

Apple rejections stung because I took them personally. But they were feedback. Next time, I'll treat each rejection as a constraint that clarifies design, not a setback.

What surprised us 2

KEY TAKEAWAYS

What this taught me

Takeaway 1
Constraints are design briefs

No native devs + no budget = build a wrapper. Constraint forced clarity. We didn't waste time on things that didn't matter.

Takeaway 2
External constraints are requirements

Apple's demands were painful. But they were feedback. Users need privacy. Apps need account deletion. Listen to constraints. Design for them.

Takeaway 3
Autonomy requires clarity

I owned design decisions, but not alone. The best ideas came from friction with engineering and product. Seek that tension.

Takeaway 4
Speed over perfection, not over intention

We shipped Android in 6 weeks, not perfectly, but intentionally. Every pattern served a purpose. Perfection would have delayed us months.

Takeaway 5
Storm from the first point

Enter at full intensity. Ship. Learn. Ship again.

Takeaway 6
Measure what moves business

Conversion rate. Retention. LTV. ABV by platform. These shaped decisions. Pretty UI means nothing if users don't book.
